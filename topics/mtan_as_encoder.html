14  ·  Attention Over Time

Many Streams, One Clock

Using the module as an encoder underneath other models.

The previous chapter built mTAN as half of a variational autoencoder, because that is how the paper presents it. But the encoder does not depend on anything wrapped around it. Take away the latent variables, the decoder and the ELBO, and what is left is a function that accepts a ragged, partially observed record together with a list of times we choose, and returns a rectangle.

That is worth having on its own, because one of the hard parts of irregular sampling was getting the measurements into a common shape without distorting the channels. The common architectures assume a regular sequence: a transformer wants tokens, a recurrent network wants steps, a convolution wants a grid. The usual way to give them one is to bin / impute, and section 08 explained why that quietly fabricates measurements. mTAN satisfies the same requirement without fabricating anything, because a reference point is a question rather than a container. So it makes sense to stop treating mTAN as a model and start treating it as a front end, this way it does the one job the downstream architecture cannot do for itself.

Contrast the two other ways of reaching a fixed shape. Binning reaches it by discarding resolution and then filling the holes. Pooling over a variable-length sequence reaches it by throwing the time axis away. The reference grid keeps the time axis, keeps whatever resolution the data supports, and invents nothing. The array is $K \times J$ whether the record holds 30 observations or 3000, whether they arrived every minute or four times in two days, whether every channel is present or only one, and downstream code needs to know none of that.

This chapter takes that and pushes it further. The record does not have to be $D$ scalar channels on one clock. It can be several streams, in different formats, arriving at different rates, each needing its own encoder before mTAN ever sees them.

The general setting

The previous chapter assumed the record was $D$ scalar channels sharing one clock. That assumption is not structurally necessary, and dropping it is where the module stops being a model of vital signs and starts being a general way to encode time.

Take the general situation. Information reaches the model through $B$ streams. Each stream arrives at its own times and rate, and what arrives is not necessarily a number. For instance, it might be a window of a waveform, an image, a span of text. So each stream comes with its own encoder $f_b$, which turns one arrival into a vector:

$$\mathbf{v}^{(b)}_i = f_b\big(u^{(b)}_i\big) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{1 \times D_b}, \qquad i = 1, \dots, L_b, \qquad b = 1, \dots, B \tag{E15}$$

Now look at what that produced. A list of times, and at each of them a vector of $D_b$ numbers. That is exactly the object chapter 13 started from, with $D_b$ channels that happen to share one observation-time set and one mask column.

The one move that makes this work

A stream that emits a $D_b$-vector at time $t$ is $D_b$ channels observed at $t$. Nothing in equations (E1) to (E7) ever asked what a channel meant. $D$ was only ever a count of value columns, and $x_{i,d}$ was only ever a number sitting in one of them. Feed the module the coordinates of an encoder's output instead of readings off a monitor and every line of the derivation still holds.

Two kinds of stream

Streams sort into two kinds:

  • Scalar streams. Vital signs, laboratory values, anything that arrives as a number. Here $f_b$ is the identity, because a reading is already a number, and $D_b$ counts how many distinct quantities are being measured. Each coordinate means something on its own: $x_{i,d}$ is a heart rate or a lactate for example. The important structural fact is that the mask has holes in it. At a given timestamp the heart rate can be present while the lactate is not, so each of the $D_b$ channels runs its own softmax over its own subset of the times, and no two of those subsets need agree.
  • Pre-embedded streams. A clinical note, a radiograph, a diagnosis code, a window of a waveform. Here $f_b$ is a real encoder: a language model, a vision backbone, an embedding table. It emits one dense vector each time something arrives, and $D_b$ is simply the width of whatever that encoder outputs. Individual coordinates mean nothing on their own; coordinate 412 of a note embedding is not a measurement of anything. And the mask is different in kind, saying only whether the thing was recorded at all, so it is constant across the whole width. You never have half a note. All $D_b$ coordinates share one index set, so all $D_b$ softmaxes are identical, and the attention weights computed for coordinate 1 are reused verbatim for coordinate 768.

Two words in that table are doing a lot of work, so here they are with a picture.

  • Partially observed is what a scalar stream's mask looks like.
    • Take one patient with a heart rate, a temperature, a lactate and a blood CO₂. A monitor writes the heart rate constantly, a nurse charts the temperature on their rounds, and the lactate and the CO₂ are laboratory tests ordered by different people for different reasons.
    • So at any given moment some channels have a value and the rest do not, and there may be no moment at all at which every channel does.
    • Read the mask down a column and you get that channel's own set of observation times; read the next column and you get a different set. Nothing lines up, and (E4) does not requires that, because each channel normalises over its own column and never looks at another.
  • Striped is what a pre-embedded stream's mask looks like.
    • Stay with the same patient and look at their notes instead. A note was written at 6.3 hours, or it was not; there is no state in which coordinate 3 of that note exists and coordinate 4 does not.
    • So the mask is really one bit per moment, copied sideways across all 768 coordinates (the note embedding representation). Every column then has the same set of observed rows, all $D_b$ softmaxes agree, and the module is doing one interpolation applied $D_b$ times.

What a striped stream lets you skip

The two shapes are not just a visual difference. They change what (E4) actually computes, and it is worth pinning down, because the per-channel machinery of section 12 was built for the partially observed case and a striped stream does not need it. Recall the weights from (E4) and watch the upper limit of the sum:

$$\kappa_{h,d}\big(r_k,\, t_{i,d}\big) = \frac{\exp e_{h,d}(r_k,\, t_{i,d})}{\displaystyle\sum_{i'=1}^{\;L_d} \exp e_{h,d}(r_k,\, t_{i',d})} \tag{E4}$$

On a partially observed stream, that $L_d$ genuinely depends on the channel, because channel $d$ was observed at its own times. This is the figure section 12 shows in which we have one reference point and a different of available measurements to read for every channel. $D_b$ distinct distributions come out, and the weight tensor has a channel axis it cannot lose.

On a striped stream every channel has the same index set, because the mask was one bit per moment, copied sideways. So the $d$ in $\kappa_{h,d}$ has nothing left to vary over. Thus, for every $d$ we have:

$$\begin{aligned} \quad \kappa_{h,d} = \kappa_h\big(r_k,\, t_i\big) &= \frac{\exp e_h(r_k,\, t_i)} {\displaystyle\sum_{i'=1}^{\;L_b} \exp e_h(r_k,\, t_{i'})} \\[6pt] \hat{x}_{h,d}(r_k) &= \sum_{i=1}^{L_b} \kappa_h\big(r_k,\, t_i\big)\, v_{i,d} \end{aligned} \tag{E19}$$

One distribution per $(h, k)$, applied to every coordinate. Three things follow, and they are the answer to what gets skipped.

  1. One softmax instead of $D_b$. We normalise once and reuse the result across the width, rather than running the same arithmetic 768 times over 768 identical index sets.
  2. The weight tensor loses an axis. It is $H \times K \times L_b$ rather than $H \times K \times L_b \times D_b$.
  3. (E5) becomes an ordinary matrix multiply. Weights of shape $K \times L_b$ times values of shape $L_b \times D_b$ gives $K \times D_b$, in one call, with no per-channel anything.

Which is worth saying plainly: on a pre-embedded stream, mTAND collapses to ordinary masked attention with unprojected values. The query is still a time and the key is still a time, so it is still doing the job of section 10, but the per-channel apparatus that section 12 needed has quietly become a no-op.

The streams disagree about three things:

  1. When they arrive. No two streams necessarily share a timestamp, and there is probably no instant at which all of them exist.
  2. How often. $L_b$ can be two thousand for one stream and three for another.
  3. How wide. $D_b$ can be $3$ for a handful of scalars and $768$ for the output of a large encoder.

Run each stream through its own module, and query every one of them at the same reference grid:

$$A_b = \operatorname{mTAND}_b\big(\mathbf{r},\, \mathcal{S}_b\big) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{K \times J_b} \tag{E16}$$

The first two disagreements are now gone. Every stream comes back with $K$ rows, indexed by the same $K$ times, however it arrived. The third disagreement does not need settling at all, because a differing $J_b$ is just a differing number of columns, and columns concatenate:

$$A = \big[\, A_1 \;\|\; A_2 \;\|\; \cdots \;\|\; A_B \,\big] \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{K \times \sum_b J_b} \tag{E17}$$

The reference grid is the common language. Each stream $b$ keeps its own encoder and its own vocabulary; what they are all made to agree on is when. And $A$ is an ordinary array. A transformer, a recurrent network, a convolution, a linear probe all can read it, and none of them ever learns that the input was irregular or that it was multimodal.

What is shared, and what is not

shared by every stream private to stream $b$
the reference grid $\mathbf{r}$, and therefore $K$ the encoder $f_b$
the time embedding $\boldsymbol{\phi}_h$, usually the projections $W_q^h$, $W_k^h$
the number of heads $H$, usually the mixing tensor $U_b$
nothing else its observation times, its mask, $L_b$, $D_b$, $J_b$

Sharing $\boldsymbol{\phi}_h$ is a choice rather than a requirement. It is free, because (E4) never compares a key of one stream against a key of another, so a stream only needs its own query and its own keys to agree with each other. What sharing buys is a single vocabulary for time, so that "around hour six" means the same thing in every stream. What it costs is the ability to give a stream its own timescales, which matters if one of them moves orders of magnitude faster than the rest.

The two places things mix

Worth separating, because they are easy to run together and they happen at different points.

  • Within a stream, at $U_b$ in (E6). The $D_b$ channels of one stream and the $H$ heads are combined into $J_b$ numbers. This is the mixing of section 12.
  • Across streams, at the concatenation in (E17), or at whatever the downstream model does with the concatenated array. Cross-attention between the blocks of $A$ is the obvious next thing to try, and it is available precisely because the blocks now have the same number of rows.

Neither of them required the streams to be aligned. That is the whole point, and it is the same argument section 04 made about channels, now applied to whole modalities. The streams stay independent exactly where alignment would have been required, and become joint everywhere after.

A stream that is not there

If a record simply has no instances of stream $b$, then $L_b = 0$, its softmax has nothing to normalise over, and by by the masked-normalisation argument of chapter 13, its block $A_b$ comes out as zeros of shape $K \times J_b$. The concatenated array keeps exactly the same shape it would have had. A downstream model trained on records where the stream is sometimes present and sometimes absent sees a constant-shaped input either way, and can learn what a zero block means.

So whole-modality missingness and single-reading missingness turn out to be one mechanism at two scales, and the mechanism is the range of a sum.

Read the diagram right to left and the claim of this chapter is visible in one line. Everything on the right is ordinary in the sense that it's a rectangle of fixed size, regularly indexed, that any architecture can consume. Everything on the left is the "mess" that real measurement produces. The module is the only thing between them, and all it needed in order to do that job was the observation that a query does not have to come from the data, and a value does not have to be a number.

So what is the attention actually doing to a note?

Worth answering directly, because the explanation so far makes it sound like the attention is barely working. It is doing exactly as much work as before.

Keep the same patient and give them three streams: their vitals and labs, their notes, and the diagnosis codes from prior admissions. The scalar stream has been covered at length, so take it as given that it arrives as $K \times J_1$. Look at the notes instead. Three were written, at 1.0 h, 6.3 h and 30 h, and a language model turned each into 768 numbers. We want five rows, at 0, 12, 24, 36 and 48 hours, because that is the grid every other stream is being resolved onto.

There is no note at hour 12. There is no note at hour 24, 36 or 48 either. Every row we want is a row nobody wrote. That is the problem the attention solves, and it solves it the same way it solved it for the lactate: by asking how close hour 12 is to each of the three moments a note exists, and blending.

Read the second row of that weight matrix. The row for hour 12 is $(0.14,\; 0.63,\; 0.23)$, so

$$\text{row}_{12\text{h}} = 0.14\,\mathbf{v}_{1.0\text{h}} + 0.63\,\mathbf{v}_{6.3\text{h}} + 0.23\,\mathbf{v}_{30\text{h}}$$

a 768-dimensional vector that leans on the 6.3 hour note because it is nearest, and that is a perfectly sensible answer to "what were the notes saying around hour 12". It did not exist in the input. The attention manufactured a row for a moment at which nobody wrote anything, which is the entire job, and is exactly what it did for the lactate in chapter 13.

What is different is only that this weight matrix is one matrix. Coordinate 1 of the note embedding and coordinate 768 get the same $(0.14, 0.63, 0.23)$, because they arrived at the same instant. On the scalar stream the equivalent picture is four different weight matrices, one per channel, because the four channels arrived at four different sets of instants.

The diagnosis stream is the same story with different numbers. Whatever prior codes the patient has, an embedding table turns each into a vector, they arrive at whatever times they arrive, one weight matrix resolves them onto the same five rows, and out comes $K \times J_3$. Then all three blocks sit side by side:

$$\underbrace{K \times J_1}_{\text{vitals and labs}} \;\Big|\; \underbrace{K \times J_2}_{\text{notes}} \;\Big|\; \underbrace{K \times J_3}_{\text{prior diagnoses}} \;=\; K \times (J_1 + J_2 + J_3)$$

Three streams that shared no timestamp, no width and no sampling rate. Their sole point of agreemeement is the reference grid.

Interpolation is not the only way in

Section 08 argued that forward filling a record fabricates measurements nobody took, and that argument stands. But it does not reach the whole case, because forward fill and mTAND fail in opposite directions, and which failure you prefer depends on the question.

  • Across a long gap, mTAND is confidently wrong. The softmax sums to one whatever the gap, so weight lands on whatever distant observations exist and a smooth, plausible number comes out with no indication of how far it reached. It also reaches forwards, so the value at hour 40 can be built partly from a measurement taken at hour 44.
  • Across a long gap, forward fill is obviously wrong. It freezes the last value and holds it flat. That is a worse estimate, but it is wrong in a predictable direction, it never reads the future, and it carries an age you can hand to the model alongside it.

Zhang, Li, Chen, Yan and Petzold, Improving Medical Predictions by Irregular Multimodal Electronic Health Records Modeling, ICML 2023, make the obvious move: do not choose. Run both, and learn a gate that decides how much of each to use.

Their module takes the same stream twice. One branch discretises it onto the same regular grid, forward fills the empty slots and reads the result with a convolution. The other branch is mTAND as we have it. Both land on the reference grid, so both come out $K \times J_b$, and two arrays of the same shape can simply be blended:

$$\begin{aligned} G = \sigma\Big( f\big(\big[\, A^{\text{fill}}_b \;\|\; A^{\text{attn}}_b \,\big]\big) \Big), \\[6pt] A_b = G \odot A^{\text{fill}}_b + (1 - G) \odot A^{\text{attn}}_b \end{aligned} \tag{E18}$$

with $f$ a small network, $\sigma$ a sigmoid and $\odot$ an elementwise product. The gate can be given three granularities, and the choice is a real one: a single scalar per record, one weight per reference point ($K$ of them), or one weight per reference point and output coordinate ($K \times J_b$). The last is the most expressive and the hardest to train.

Notice what makes (E18) even writable. Two entirely different treatments of the same stream, one that invents values on a grid and one that refuses to, become addable only because they were both resolved onto the same $K$ reference points. The grid is not just an interface to the downstream model, it is an interface between rival representations of the same stream.

What the gate buys, concretely

Take one patient with two scalar channels: a heart rate off a bedside monitor, and a lactate drawn twice in two days.

On the heart rate the two branches agree. There is an observation within a minute or two of every reference point, so the interpolation and the fill both return essentially the last reading. The gate is free to pick either, and whichever it picks costs nothing.

On the lactate at hour 40 they disagree completely. With draws at hour 6 and hour 44, mTAND returns a blend of the two, which is the better estimate of what the lactate actually was, and which used a measurement from four hours in the future. Forward fill returns the hour-6 value and nothing else, which is a worse estimate and an honest one.

A per-coordinate gate can learn exactly that split: lean on mTAND where the record is dense, lean on the fill where it is sparse. And the choice is not only about accuracy. If the task is a real-time alert then the mTAND branch is reading the future and cannot be deployed as it stands, so the gate is also a place to encode what the model is allowed to know.
