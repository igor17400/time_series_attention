13  ·  Attention Over Time

The mTAN Network

The same module run in both directions.

Where we are

Everything so far has been building towards a single paper. Multi-Time Attention Networks for Irregularly Sampled Time Series, by Satya Narayan Shukla and Benjamin Marlin, presented at ICLR 2021, is the work that motivated this tutorial. It takes a mechanism built so that a sentence could look at itself and turns it into a way of reading time.

Here is what has been already introduced.

  • A record that is three lists of different lengths with no shared timestamps, written down formally in section 07.
  • A reason not to repair it into a rectangle, from section 08.
  • A learned embedding that turns any real time into a vector, from section 09.
  • An attention whose scores are built from time alone, which turns out to be kernel regression with a learned kernel, from section 10.
  • A grid of reference points that we choose rather than read off the data, from section 11.
  • One softmax per channel and a mixing tensor, from section 12.

This chapter puts them together into one architecture. What is new is the assembly: the encoder, the decoder, the objective, and the shape of every tensor that passes between them. mTAN is presented here as an encoder for incomplete and multimodal series. The decoder gets a full section, because the architecture is not comprehensible without it and because the symmetry between the two is the cleanest way to hold the whole thing in mind. But the question the chapter is answering is what you get when you put an irregular, partially observed, possibly multimodal record in at one end and want a fixed size representation out at the other.

The problem, stated once and properly

A record has $D$ channels. Channel $d$ carries its own list of $L_d$ observations, each one a time and a value:

$$\mathcal{S}_d = \big\{ (t_{i,d},\; x_{i,d}) \big\}_{i=1}^{L_d}, \qquad t_{1,d} < t_{2,d} < \cdots < t_{L_d,\, d}, \qquad \mathcal{S} = (\mathcal{S}_1, \dots, \mathcal{S}_D)$$

Both $t_{i,d}$ and $x_{i,d}$ are scalars. $\mathcal{S}_d$ is a set of $L_d$ pairs and $\mathcal{S}$ is the whole record. This is section 07 unchanged.

Three things are true of this object and each of them breaks something a standard model assumes.

  1. $L_d$ varies with $d$. The patient example we have been carrying all along has $D = 3$ with $L_1 \approx 2000$ pulse readings, $L_2 = 11$ temperatures and $L_3 = 4$ lactates. There is no single sequence length.
  2. The spacings $t_{i+1,d} - t_{i,d}$ are not constant. Not within a channel and not across channels. So an index is not a clock, which is section 06.
  3. The channels are not aligned. Pick an instant and at most one channel has a reading there. There is no time at which a complete $D$-vector exists.

THe lack of channel alignment drives the most significant architectural failure. We must define the exact mechanism behind this breakdown. The assumption of non-alignment reflects the practical reality of the data rather than an inviolable rule. That is, sometimes two channels will register a shared timestamp and for those cases the model should process this without issue. The problem arises from the inability to guarantee alignment across the entire record. Any standard architecture that demands temporal alignment is forced to synthetically produce it. That artificial manufacturing phase represents the exact structural rejection in section 08.

The union grid and the mask

An implementation cannot hold $D$ ragged lists in a tensor, so it holds one padded time axis and a mask. Let

$$\mathcal{T} = \bigcup_{d=1}^{D} \{\, t_{1,d}, \dots, t_{L_d,\,d} \,\} = \{\, \tau_1 < \tau_2 < \cdots < \tau_L \,\}$$

be the union of every observation time in the record, of size $L$, and define the mask

$$m_{\ell,d} = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if channel } d \text{ was measured at } \tau_\ell \\[4pt] 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \qquad M \in \{0,1\}^{L \times D}$$

with the value array $X \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times D}$ holding $x_{i,d}$ wherever $m_{\ell,d} = 1$ and anything at all (zero, by convention) wherever it is $0$. Note that $\sum_{\ell} m_{\ell,d} = L_d$, so the mask carries exactly the per-channel counts. An interesting part to highlight is that the equations don't actually need $\mathcal{T}$ or $M$, the sums below all run over $i = 1, \dots, L_d$ directly. The masks exist so that a GPU can do the work.

The multimodal case is this case. Replace "channel" with "modality" and every word above still holds. Different modalities arrive at different rates, for instance, a waveform at 500 Hz, a laboratory result four times in two days, a clinical note once a shift. They have different missingness patterns, including the pattern where an entire modality is absent from a record, which is just $L_d = 0$ for every column belonging to it. The one thing that does not carry over automatically is that a modality need not be one scalar per timestamp, and later subsections deals with that.

Learned time embeddings

The module needs a way to turn a time into something comparable. Section 09 built one. The only thing this chapter adds is the head index, the paper carries $H$ embedding functions at once, not one.

$$\boldsymbol{\phi}_h(t)[m] = \begin{cases} \omega_{0,h}\, t + \alpha_{0,h}, & m = 0 \\[4pt] \sin\!\big(\omega_{m,h}\, t + \alpha_{m,h}\big), & 1 \leq m < d_r \end{cases} \tag{E1}$$

In plain language this equation reads as follows: "coordinate $m$ of head $h$'s embedding of the time $t$ is a sine of a learned frequency and phase, except coordinate $0$, which is a straight line in $t$".

  • In: $t$, one scalar. Out: $\boldsymbol{\phi}_h(t)$, a row vector of shape $1 \times d_r$, where $d_r$ is the embedding width.
  • Learned: $\omega_{m,h}$ and $\alpha_{m,h}$ for $m = 0, \dots, d_r - 1$ and $h = 1, \dots, H$. That is $2\,d_r\,H$ scalars.
  • The $d_r - 1$ periodic coordinates are bounded in $[-1, 1]$ and say where in a rhythm. The one linear coordinate is unbounded and says how far through.

The reason a purely periodic basis is not enough is the argument from section 09.

Each head has its own embedding function. That is what equation (E1) says, with $h$ on both $\omega$ and $\alpha$. It matters because the frequencies are what a head's notion of closeness is built out of, so giving each head its own frequencies is what lets one head care about the last twenty minutes while another cares about the whole stay, which is the picture in section 12.

One observation on (E1)

The released implementation does not build $H$ separate embeddings. It builds one $d_r$-wide embedding, projects it once, and splits the projection into $H$ slices of width $d_r/H$, which is ordinary multi-head attention. So in the code the heads share $\omega$ and $\alpha$ and differ only in which slice of the projection they get.

The mTAND module in full

Reference points

Fix a list of $K$ times we intend to ask about, $\mathbf{r} = [\,r_1, \dots, r_K\,]$, a row vector of shape $1 \times K$. These are the reference points of section 11. Two standard choices: an evenly spaced grid over the observation window, which is what the paper uses for classification with $K = 128$; or $\rho(\mathcal{S})$, the union $\mathcal{T}$ of every time at which any channel was observed, which makes the grid adapt to the record.

A fixed reference grid is what converts an irregular problem into a regular one. Meaning that the output length of an attention layer is set by the number of queries, not by the number of keys. When we choose the queries and we are choosing the output shape. That single fact is why a patient with 2000 readings and a patient with 30 leave this module in exactly the same shape.

The attention, and what the query, key and value are

For a head $h$ and a channel $d$, project the embedded query time and the embedded observation time:

$$\mathbf{q}_h(t) = \boldsymbol{\phi}_h(t)\, W_q^{h}, \qquad \mathbf{k}_h(t_{i,d}) = \boldsymbol{\phi}_h(t_{i,d})\, W_k^{h} \tag{E2}$$

with $W_q^{h}, W_k^{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_r \times d_k}$ and $d_k \leq d_r$, so both $\mathbf{q}_h(t)$ and $\mathbf{k}_h(t_{i,d})$ have shape $1 \times d_k$. Scoring them yields:

$$e_{h,d}\big(t,\, t_{i,d}\big) = \frac{\mathbf{q}_h(t)\, \mathbf{k}_h(t_{i,d})^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}} = \frac{\boldsymbol{\phi}_h(t)\, W_q^{h}\, W_k^{h\top}\, \boldsymbol{\phi}_h(t_{i,d})^{\top}} {\sqrt{d_k}} \tag{E3}$$

One scalar per $(h, d, i, t)$. The chain of shapes is $(1 \times d_r)(d_r \times d_k)(d_k \times d_r)(d_r \times 1)$, which is $1 \times 1$. The $\sqrt{d_k}$ is the usual scaling from section 10.

This is not the usual query, key, value triple

  • The query is $\boldsymbol{\phi}_h(t)W_q^{h}$, built from a time we chose. It does not come from the data at all.
  • The key is $\boldsymbol{\phi}_h(t_{i,d})W_k^{h}$, built from when an observation happened and from nothing else.
  • The value is $x_{i,d}$. The measurement itself. A raw scalar in the units of the data. There is no $W_v$ and there is no projection. This is the real departure from usual attention, where the key and the value are two projections of one token, so what a token says helps decide how much of it gets read. Here they come from two different objects, the timestamp and the reading, and only the timestamp is allowed near the score.

The masked normalisation

As we have introduced before, normalise the scores over the observations that channel $d$ actually has, and read off a weighted average of what was measured:

$$\kappa_{h,d}\big(t,\, t_{i,d}\big) = \frac{\exp e_{h,d}(t,\, t_{i,d})}{\displaystyle\sum_{i'=1}^{L_d} \exp e_{h,d}(t,\, t_{i',d})} \tag{E4}$$
$$\hat{x}_{h,d}(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{L_d} \kappa_{h,d}\big(t,\, t_{i,d}\big)\, x_{i,d} \tag{E5}$$

Every variable in the equations above is a scalar. The entire masking mechanism is encapsulated within the upper limit of those two sums. The limit is specifically $L_d$ rather than a global $L$. Because the summation iterates exclusively over one channel's own recorded observations, the system never queries a channel at a time when no measurement occurred. As a consequence, the model does not actually requires data imputation. This mechenism represents the argument of section 12, and it serves as the architectural solution for missingness.

In the dense form, with the union grid and the mask introduced above, the same thing is written as

$$\kappa_{h,d}\big(t,\, \tau_\ell\big) = \frac{m_{\ell,d} \exp e_{h,d}(t,\, \tau_\ell)}{\displaystyle\sum_{\ell'=1}^{L} m_{\ell',d} \exp e_{h,d}(t,\, \tau_{\ell'})} \tag{E4$'$}$$

which is what an implementation computes by adding a large negative number (e.g., in pytorch attention_scores.masked_fill(mask, float('-inf')))) to the masked logits before the softmax rather than by multiplying after it.

Heads, and what varies across them

Everything from (E1) to (E5) is repeated for $h = 1, \dots, H$. What varies across heads is the embedding parameters $\omega_{m,h}, \alpha_{m,h}$ and the projections $W_q^{h}, W_k^{h}$.

What does not vary: the values $x_{i,d}$, the observation times, the mask, and the reference grid. A head is a different opinion about which times are close to which, applied to the same evidence.

Folding the head and channel axes

After (E5) we hold $H \times D$ scalars for each query time. The last step mixes them into $J$ numbers:

$$\operatorname{mTAN}(t,\, \mathcal{S})[j] = \sum_{h=1}^{H} \sum_{d=1}^{D} \hat{x}_{h,d}(t)\, U_{h,d,j} \tag{E6}$$

with $U \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times D \times J}$. Read as a matrix multiplication, flatten the $(h,d)$ pair into one axis of length $HD$ and (E6) is a row vector of length $HD$ times an $HD \times J$ matrix, giving a row vector of length $J$. This is the $U$ of section 12, and it is the only place in the module where different channels are combined.

Evaluate (E6) at each reference point and stack the results:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{mTAND}(\mathbf{r},\, \mathcal{S})[k,:] &\;=\; \operatorname{mTAN}(r_k,\, \mathcal{S}) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{1 \times J} \\[4pt] \operatorname{mTAND}(\mathbf{r},\, \mathcal{S}) &\;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{K \times J} \end{aligned} \tag{E7}$$

Where the D in mTAND comes from

$\operatorname{mTAN}(t, \mathcal{S})$ is a function of one query time. We hand it a single instant and it hands back $J$ numbers, and it will do that for any real $t$, so it is a continuous object with no shape and no length. Nothing built out of matrices can consume it.

$\operatorname{mTAND}$ is that same function discretised: evaluated at the finite list $\mathbf{r}$ and stacked into an array. That is the whole of the difference, and the D stands for discretised. It is a materialised mTAN, not a different mechanism.

Pay attention: the $D$ in mTAND has nothing to do with $D$, the number of channels, which appears two equations earlier in (E6) and never leaves. One is a word, the other is a count.

One shape calcualtion example for $D = 3$, $H = 4$, $K = 128$:

after object shape example
input $X$, $M$ on the union grid $L \times D$ each $2015 \times 3$
(E1) queries embedded $H \times K \times d_r$ $4 \times 128 \times 128$
(E1) keys embedded $H \times L \times d_r$ $4 \times 2015 \times 128$

The weight tensor at (E4) is the big one, and the reason is that the $L$ axis is the union of every channel's times while the softmax is per channel, so the same $H \times K \times L$ block of logits is reused $D$ times under $D$ different masks. That is a memory cost paid to make the whole thing one matrix multiplication.

Example: one reference point, one channel, one head

Let's walk through one example. Take the lactate channel, $d = 3$, with its four readings at minutes 15, 380, 1120 and 2650 carrying values $2.4$, $3.1$, $4.6$ and $2.8$. Take the reference point $r_2 = 720$ (hour 12) and the head $h = 1$. Then $L_3 = 4$ and the computation is nine numbers long.

  1. Embed the query time $\boldsymbol{\phi}_1(720) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d_r}$ and project it $\mathbf{q}_1(720) = \boldsymbol{\phi}_1(720)W_q^{1} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d_k}$.
  2. Embed and project the four observation times, giving four keys $\mathbf{k}_1(15)$, $\mathbf{k}_1(380)$, $\mathbf{k}_1(1120)$, $\mathbf{k}_1(2650)$, each $1 \times d_k$. The lactate values play no part in this step and never will.
  3. Four dot products, scaled. Suppose they come out as
    $$\begin{aligned} e_{1,3}(720,\, 15) &= 0.29, &\qquad e_{1,3}(720,\, 380) &= 1.40, \\[2pt] e_{1,3}(720,\, 1120) &= 0.61, &\qquad e_{1,3}(720,\, 2650) &= -1.91 \end{aligned}$$
  4. Exponentiate: $1.336$, $4.055$, $1.840$, $0.148$. Sum: $7.380$. Divide:
    $$\kappa_{1,3}(720, \cdot) = (0.18,\; 0.55,\; 0.25,\; 0.02)$$
    which sums to $1$ by construction.
  5. Weighted average of the four measurements:
    $$\hat{x}_{1,3}(720) = 0.18(2.4) + 0.55(3.1) + 0.25(4.6) + 0.02(2.8) = 3.34$$

That is one of the $H \times D = 12$ scalars that reference point $r_2$ produces. The other eleven are the same five steps with a different $h$, a different $d$, or both. Equation (E6) then turns those twelve numbers into $J = 64$ numbers, and $r_2$ is finished.

Notice what $3.34$ is. It lies between $2.4$ and $4.6$, and it has to, because a convex combination of the four measurements cannot leave their range. The module interpolates and it does not extrapolate beyond the values it was given, however far in time we ask.

Another example: two channels, five reference points

Now the same thing at a larger scale with two channels with deliberately different observation patterns, and put $K = 5$ reference points on the hour marks $0$, $12$, $24$, $36$, $48$.

  • Channel 1, lactate. $L_1 = 4$, at hours $0.25$, $6.3$, $18.7$, $44.2$, values $2.4$, $3.1$, $4.6$, $2.8$. Sparse and spread across the whole stay.
  • Channel 2, temperature. $L_2 = 5$, at hours $1$, $5$, $9$, $21$, $26$, values $37.1$, $37.4$, $38.2$, $38.9$, $39.4$. Clustered early, and then nothing at all for the last 22 hours.

The two attention weight matrices are $5 \times 4$ and $5 \times 5$. They have different widths, they are never compared to one another, and each row of each of them sums to one. The numbers below are illustrative, chosen to show the shape a trained kernel takes rather than read off a trained model, but the row sums and the qualitative structure are the real thing.

Four things to read off those two tables.

  1. The attention mass naturally follows the queries along the diagonal. Each reference point correctly assigns the highest weight to the measurements closest to its own timestamp. No explicit rule enforced this structure, this happens entirely from the learned frequencies.
  2. The two matrices have different widths and never meet. No step required the lactate at 6.3 hours and the temperature at 5 hours to be reconciled, aligned, binned or filled. They are combined for the first time at (E6), after both have been resolved onto the same five reference points.
  3. Nothing was invented. Every entry in the last column is a convex combination of numbers a clinician wrote down. Compare the $48 \times 3$ matrix of section 08, of which 44 lactate cells were copies.
  4. And here is the failure. Look at the temperature rows for 36 hours and 48 hours: $39.09$ and $39.12$. Twelve hours apart, and practically the same answer, because after hour 26 there is nothing left to attend to and the softmax has nowhere else to put its mass. The output is a confident number with no attached warning that it was reached across a 22 hour gap.

The module, drawn

Every arrow carries the shape of what flows along it, and every block carries the equation it implements.

The encoder

The encoder turns the record into something rectangular and then into something probabilistic. Three steps.

Step one. Run the module at the reference points, which is (E7) with the observations as keys and values:

$$A^{\text{enc}}_{\text{TAN}} = \operatorname{mTAND}^{\text{enc}}(\mathbf{r},\, \mathcal{S}) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{K \times J}$$

We write $A$ for the arrays that carry a value per reference point, with a superscript saying which half of the network they belong to and a subscript saying which stage produced them. The paper calls these $h^{\text{enc}}_{\text{TAN}}$ and so on, but the letter is changed here only because $H$ is already the number of heads. Row $k$ of this one is the $J$ numbers describing the record as seen from time $r_k$.

Step two. Read that array along the reference axis with a bidirectional GRU (given that this tutorial doesn't go into GRUs, here is a reference to it.):

$$A^{\text{enc}}_{\text{RNN}} = \operatorname{biGRU}\big(A^{\text{enc}}_{\text{TAN}}\big) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{K \times 2g} \tag{E8}$$

The GRU is bidirectional: one pass runs forward along the reference grid, $r_1$ to $r_K$, and a second runs backward, $r_K$ to $r_1$. Each pass carries its own hidden state of width $g$, and the two are concatenated at every reference point, which is where the $2g$ comes from. So $r_k$ ends up knowing about the whole stay on both sides of it, not only what came before.

This is the step where a reference point comes to know about its neighbours rather than only about the observations it attended to. It is also, incidentally, where content finally gets to influence anything, since (E3) refused to let it influence the weights.

Step three. A small network maps each row to the parameters of a Gaussian, and a latent vector is drawn there:

$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\mu}_k &= f_\mu\big(A^{\text{enc}}_{\text{RNN}}[k,:]\big), \qquad \boldsymbol{\sigma}_k^2 = \exp f_\sigma\big(A^{\text{enc}}_{\text{RNN}}[k,:]\big) \\[4pt] \mathbf{z}_k &\sim \mathcal{N}\big(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k,\; \operatorname{diag}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_k^2\big) \end{aligned} \tag{E9}$$

where $d_z$ is the latent width, so $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\sigma}_k, \mathbf{z}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d_z}$, and $f_\mu, f_\sigma$ are two heads off one two-layer network $\mathbb{R}^{2g} \to \mathbb{R}^{2 d_z}$.

Equation (E9) is evaluated once per reference point, so it produces $K$ separate draws, each a row of $d_z$ numbers. Collect those $K$ rows into a single array, one row per reference point in time order and it yields $Z$:

$$Z = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{z}_1 \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{z}_K \end{bmatrix} \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{K \times d_z}$$

What the latent indexes over

It is crucial to highlight that there are $K$ latent vectors, one per reference point, not one latent vector for the record. $Z$ is a sequence, and its index is time. The record is not compressed into a single summary and then expanded again, it is compressed into a sequence of summaries, each responsible for its own stretch of the stay.

That is a deliberate choice and the paper calls it a temporally distributed latent representation. It is what lets the model hold on to something that happened at hour 6 and had stopped by hour 30. A single global latent has to spend capacity remembering when; a sequence of latents gets it for free from the index.

Note also what $K$ is now doing. It is simultaneously the resolution of the interpolation, the length of the RNN sequence, and the length of the latent. One hyperparameter, three jobs.

The decoder, and the swap

The decoder runs the same three steps in the other order and, more importantly, runs the module with its roles exchanged. Start from latents, either drawn from the prior $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$ when generating or from (E9) when reconstructing.

$$A^{\text{dec}}_{\text{RNN}} = \operatorname{biGRU}^{\text{dec}}(Z) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{K \times 2g} \tag{E10}$$

The latents are independent across $k$ under the prior, so this step exists to give them temporal dependence before anything is read off them. Then the module again, at whatever times we want output for:

$$A^{\text{dec}}_{\text{TAN}} = \operatorname{mTAND}^{\text{dec}}\big(\mathbf{t},\, A^{\text{dec}}_{\text{RNN}}\big) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{T \times J'} \tag{E11}$$

where $\mathbf{t} = [t_1, \dots, t_T]$ is the list of times we are asking about, and $J'$ is the width of the decoder module's output, which is a free hyperparameter exactly as $J$ was. Finally a fully connected map, applied independently at each output time, produces a mean per channel:

$$x_{i,d} \sim \mathcal{N}\Big(f^{\text{dec}} \big(A^{\text{dec}}_{\text{TAN}}[i,:]\big)[d], \; \sigma^2 \Big), \qquad f^{\text{dec}} : \mathbb{R}^{J'} \to \mathbb{R}^{D} \tag{E12}$$

with $\sigma^2$ a fixed constant, not learned.

The symmetry encoder and decoder

(E11) is (E7) the same function with three substitutions.

key $\boldsymbol{\phi}_h(t_{i,d})$, an observation time. $L$ of them $\boldsymbol{\phi}_h(r_k)$, a reference point. $K$ of them
value $x_{i,d}$, a measurement. $D$ channels $A^{\text{dec}}_{\text{RNN}}[k,:]$, a hidden state. $2g$ coordinates

output $K \times J$ $T \times J'$

The training objective

The model is a variational autoencoder, so it is trained by maximising a lower bound on the likelihood of the record. Two term follow a reconstruction term saying the decoder should reproduce what was measured, and a term keeping each $\mathbf{z}_k$ close to a standard normal prior.

$$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{n} \frac{1}{\sum_{d} L_{d,n}} \Big( \mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log p(\mathbf{x}_n \mid Z, \mathbf{t}_n)\big] \;-\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\,(n)} \Big) \tag{E13}$$

where $D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\,(n)}$ is shorthand for the divergence between the posterior $q(Z \mid \mathbf{r}, \mathcal{S}_n)$ and the prior $p(Z)$. The two are calculated as follows

$$\begin{aligned} D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\,(n)} &= \sum_{k=1}^{K} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\mathbf{z}_k \mid \mathbf{r}, \mathcal{S}_n) \,\|\, p(\mathbf{z}_k)\big) \\[6pt] \log p(\mathbf{x}_n \mid Z, \mathbf{t}_n) &= \sum_{d=1}^{D} \sum_{i=1}^{L_{d,n}} \log p\big(x_{i,d,n} \mid Z, t_{i,d,n}\big) \end{aligned}$$

Three things are doing work in there.

  1. The KL factorises over reference points because the prior is independent across $k$. So it is $K$ small Gaussian KLs added up, one per reference point, not one KL over a $K d_z$-dimensional object.
  2. The reconstruction term is summed only over observations that exist. The inner sum runs to $L_{d,n}$. There is no term in the loss for the lactate at minute 300, because nothing was measured there, so the model is never trained to reproduce a value nobody recorded. This is the second and last place missingness enters the model, the first being the range of the softmax in (E4). Everywhere else it has already been dealt with.
  3. Each record is divided by its own observation count. Without the $1 / \sum_d L_{d,n}$ factor, a patient with two thousand pulse readings would contribute hundreds of times more gradient than a patient with thirty, and the model would quietly become a model of well monitored patients.

For a supervised task the latents are read by a classifier and a weighted term is added:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\text{sup}} &= \mathcal{L} + \lambda\, \mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log p_\delta(y_n \mid Z)\big] \\[4pt] y^{*} &= \arg\max_{y}\; \mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log p_\delta(y \mid Z)\big] \end{aligned} \tag{E14}$$

with $p_\delta$ a GRU over the $K$ latents followed by a small fully connected network, and $\lambda$ trading the two off.

That is the model the paper builds. The next section drops it and asks a more general question: how mTAN can be used as an encoder underneath somebody else's neural network?
